Cannabis Use: A Different Great American Smokeout

By IAWH Health  |  Great American Smokeout  |  November 19, 2026

Fewer women smoke cigarettes. The smoke hasn’t cleared.

For 50 years, the Great American Smokeout has asked smokers to put down cigarettes for one day. This year, IAWH is asking a different question: if women are quitting cigarettes, what is taking their place?

The answer, for a growing number of women, is a vape pen, a gummy or a joint. This Smokeout, we want to talk about cannabis: what has changed, who it affects and what the evidence says. No scolding. Just the facts every woman deserves to have.

The good news: cigarettes are at a record low

In 2024, 9.9% of U.S. adults smoked cigarettes. That’s the first time the rate has ever dropped below 10%. In 1965, 42.4% of adults smoked.

Women led much of that progress. Among women ages 18 to 44, cigarette smoking fell by more than half in ten years, from 17.4% to 8.4%. That’s nearly 4.8 million fewer women smoking.

The twist: vaping and cannabis are filling the gap

As cigarettes fade, other habits are rising. About 7% of U.S. adults vaped nicotine in 2024, and among women of childbearing age, e-cigarette use has passed 10%.

Cannabis use is climbing even faster. Each year, a national survey asks Americans whether they used marijuana in the 12 months before they answered. In 2024, 22.3% of people ages 12 and older said yes. That’s 64.2 million people, up from 19.0% in 2021.

Women are part of that rise. Among adult women, the share who used cannabis in the past 12 months grew from 17.7% in 2021 to 21.2% in 2024.

How often people use it is changing too. In 2022, for the first time, more Americans used marijuana daily or almost daily (17.7 million) than drank alcohol that often (14.7 million). Researchers estimate that about 4 in 10 current cannabis users now use it daily or almost every day.

This is not your mother’s marijuana

THC (short for tetrahydrocannabinol) is the main compound in cannabis that produces the “high.” In 1995, cannabis seized by federal agents averaged about 4% THC. By 2022, the average had climbed to about 16%.

Concentrates, sold as oils, wax, shatter or resin, can deliver much more. Some hemp shops also sell “THCA flower,” which turns into THC when someone heats it. A product labeled “hemp” can still produce a strong high.

Women use cannabis in four main ways:

  • Smoking: dried flower in joints, pipes, water pipes (bongs) or cigar wrappers (blunts).
  • Vaping: THC oils in cartridges or vape pens.
  • Dabbing: heating a concentrated extract and inhaling the vapor.
  • Eating or drinking: gummies, baked goods and infused beverages. Edibles take longer to kick in, so people often take more before the first dose hits.

Where women get it

Legalization has put cannabis on Main Street. Most states with legal adult sales set the buying age at 21, and some allow online ordering and delivery.

A national study of adults in states with medical cannabis found that the last time people used, 42.5% bought it at a store or dispensary. Another 36% got it without paying, through sharing, gifts or home growing. The remaining 21.5% bought it from another source. (The study used 2021 survey data, so today’s numbers may differ.)

Legal does not mean safe. A product can come from a licensed store and still carry real risks for your heart, your mind and a pregnancy. Alcohol and cigarettes are legal, too.

What is cannabis use disorder, and who develops it?

Cannabis use disorder (CUD) is the medical term for problem cannabis use, including addiction. A clinician diagnoses it when cannabis causes real problems or distress and a person has trouble controlling their use. Using every day, buying legally or testing positive on a drug test does not, by itself, mean someone has CUD.

Clinicians look for at least 2 of 11 signs within the same 12 months. Two or three signs count as mild, four or five as moderate, and six or more as severe. Common signs include:

  • Trying to cut back and not being able to.
  • Using more, or for longer, than planned.
  • Strong cravings.
  • Needing more to feel the same effect.
  • Missing work, family or other responsibilities because of use.
  • Giving up activities you used to enjoy.
  • Continuing even when it’s hurting your health or your relationships.
  • Feeling irritable, anxious, restless or unable to sleep or eat when you stop.

Withdrawal is real. Symptoms usually start one to two days after stopping, peak around days two to six, and can last for weeks in heavy users. That discomfort often makes going back to cannabis feel like relief.

CDC estimates that about 3 in 10 people who use cannabis have some degree of CUD. Your risk goes up if you use daily or almost daily, started as a teen, or use high-THC products. A 2022 review in The Lancet Psychiatry linked higher-potency cannabis to more addiction and more psychosis than lower-potency products.

Among women 50 and older, moderate-to-severe cannabis use disorder rose about sevenfold between 2021 and 2024.

Women are not immune. Between 2021 and 2024, CUD among adult women rose from 4.5% to 5.6%. The more serious forms grew faster, from 1.7% to 2.5% of women. Among women with CUD, the share with a moderate or severe case rose from 37% to 45%. The sharpest jump came among women 50 and older, a group many people never think of as cannabis users.

CUD can be quiet. A woman can hold a job, raise her children and keep her friendships while still struggling with cravings and failed attempts to cut back.

The long view: what years of heavy use can do

Your heart. In a study of 430,000 adults, people who used cannabis daily had 25% higher odds of a heart attack and 42% higher odds of a stroke than non-users. The risk held even for people who had never smoked tobacco. Among men under 55 and women under 65, those most at risk of early heart disease, cannabis use raised the combined odds of heart disease, heart attack and stroke by 36%. For women already managing high blood pressure, that matters.

Your lungs. Smoking cannabis exposes your lungs to many of the same harmful chemicals as tobacco smoke. Vaping has its own link to serious lung injury.

Your mind. Daily use of high-THC cannabis carries a higher risk of psychosis. Many women use cannabis to manage stress, anxiety or sleep, but heavy use can make those problems harder to treat over time.

Your stomach. Some long-term heavy users develop cannabis hyperemesis syndrome: repeated bouts of severe nausea and vomiting that often send people to the emergency room. It usually stops only when cannabis use stops.

Most of these findings show links, not proof that cannabis caused every outcome. But the pattern across studies is consistent, and it points in one direction.

Planning a family starts before the pregnancy test.

Trying to conceive, pregnant or breastfeeding

Getting pregnant. In an NIH study of 1,228 women trying to conceive after a pregnancy loss, women who used cannabis were about 40% less likely to get pregnant in any given month. Only 62 women in the study used cannabis, so researchers call for more study. Newer IVF research also links THC to lower egg quality. Their advice to women trying to conceive: use caution.

During pregnancy. More pregnant women are using cannabis. In one large California health system, prenatal use rose from 5.5% in 2012 to 9.0% in 2022, with the highest rates among Black women and women under 25. Many use it in the first trimester to cope with nausea, stress or sleep problems.

A study of more than 316,000 pregnancies found that women who used cannabis had higher risks of:

  • Gestational hypertension (high blood pressure that starts during pregnancy): 17% higher risk.
  • Preeclampsia (a dangerous pregnancy complication with high blood pressure and organ strain): 8% higher risk.
  • Placental abruption (the placenta pulling away from the uterus too early): 19% higher risk.

For babies, a 2025 review of 51 studies covering more than 21 million pregnancies linked cannabis use in pregnancy to preterm birth, low birth weight and smaller babies, even after accounting for tobacco.

Emerging research also connects cannabis use in pregnancy with more depressive symptoms and stress among mothers, including in a 2026 study of 1,066 pregnant Black women in Michigan, Ohio and Florida, where more than 1 in 5 reported using cannabis during pregnancy. Researchers can’t yet say which comes first: the symptoms or the cannabis.

Breastfeeding. THC passes into breast milk and can stay in a mother’s body long after her last use.

What doctors now recommend. In 2025, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) stated that there is no medical reason to use cannabis during pregnancy or breastfeeding. ACOG now recommends that providers ask every patient about cannabis use before pregnancy, during pregnancy and after birth. If you’re using cannabis for nausea, anxiety, sleep or pain, tell your prenatal provider. Safer treatments exist.

Myths vs. facts

Myth: “It’s natural, so it’s safe.”

Fact: Plenty of natural substances can harm you. Today’s cannabis averages about four times the THC of the mid-1990s.

Myth: “It’s legal now, so it must be safe.”

Fact: Legal and safe are two different things. Alcohol and cigarettes are legal, too.

Myth: “You can’t get addicted to weed.”

Fact: About 3 in 10 people who use cannabis have some degree of cannabis use disorder, and stopping can cause real withdrawal.

Myth: “Vapes and gummies are the safe way to use it.”

Fact: Switching methods doesn’t remove the risk. Vapes and concentrates can deliver very high doses of THC, and edibles make it easy to take too much.

Myth: “It helps with morning sickness, so it’s fine in pregnancy.”

Fact: ACOG says there is no medical reason to use cannabis during pregnancy. Your provider can offer safer options for nausea.

Myth: “Weed doesn’t affect your heart.”

Fact: Daily users have 25% higher odds of heart attack and 42% higher odds of stroke.

If you want to cut back

You don’t have to hit bottom to ask for help. Talk with your primary care provider, or ask for a referral to a counselor or addiction specialist. Talk therapies, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and motivational counseling, help many people reduce or stop.

  • SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7, in English and Spanish)
  • Quitting cigarettes or vapes too? Call 1-800-QUIT-NOW (1-800-784-8669).
  • In crisis? Call or text 988.
